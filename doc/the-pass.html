How one ticket gets built

Lingtai takes one ticket at a time and walks it through ten steps. This page is what each step is, what you decide about it, and where a ticket goes when a step will not let it past.

The pipeline is fixed; what each step does is yours. The ten steps and their order never change — that is what makes the board, the log and this page describe one thing. What runs at each step is a plugin, and your recipe names it. Say nothing and you get the set Lingtai ships, which is a working pipeline.

One worktree is the whole of a pass. A ticket gets a branch cut from your base, and everything after that — the install, the agent, the build, the review, the merge — happens in it. Nothing is thrown away and restarted because a step said no.

The ten steps

Read it downward. The four in brass are the steps that may say no; everything that does not simply pass arrives at proposed, which is the only step that decides what happens next.

What you write

One file, on your machine, at ~/.lingtai/<project>/recipe.yml. Nothing is committed to the repository Lingtai works on.

steps:
  claim:
    - queue: { kinds: [bug, feature], backoff: 1h }
      restarts: 1        # how many times this ticket may be claimed
  admit:
    - worktree: { base: main }
  prepared:
    - run: pnpm install
      timeout: 10m
  implement:
    - agent: claude-code
      turns: 150            # inside one agent run
      wall:  1h             # inside one agent run
      rounds: 3            # how many times a refusal sends the agent back
  build:
    - run: pnpm typecheck && pnpm test
  review:
    - agent: claude-code

A step is a list of plugins, and they run in the order you write them. What the step does with the results is the step's own business: at claim the first plugin that finds a ticket wins; at prepared every one of them has to pass. A step you leave out of the file simply has nothing at it — and is still drawn, so that nothing configured and silently skipped never look alike.

Some keys are not the plugin's — they are the bounds Lingtai puts on it. A run: plugin cannot ignore its timeout, because the runner enforces it rather than the plugin honouring it. rounds and restarts work the same way, and each sits on the step it bounds.

What a pass can cost you

Four numbers, and the first three are multiplied by the fourth.

Read it as arithmetic: one pass is up to rounds + 1 agent runs, each bounded by turns and wall — and restarts is how many passes one ticket may buy. lingtai add prints the whole sum before anything runs.

Nothing here bounds what you are asked. A step that did not finish — an agent that stopped to ask, a reviewer that crashed — buys no round, because it passed no judgement on your change.

When it stops and waits for you

One way in, and one place to look.

What puts a ticket there

Every ceiling is spent — the rounds, then the restarts — and the change is still refused. Or a question came back that only you can answer: an unclear requirement, a design decision, two changes that edited the same intent differently.

Only proposed can send a ticket to you. No step reaches you on its own, which is what makes is this worth interrupting somebody over one decision in one place rather than a habit scattered through the pipeline.

What it carries with it

What refused it and why, in the words the step used — the findings, the build's output, the paths that conflicted. You should never have to open a log to learn why a ticket is waiting.

And end still runs. Closing the ticket yourself is an outcome like any other, and the step that tidies up runs on every outcome rather than only on the happy one.
